At the heart of my practice

Two ways in when words run out.

EMDR and art therapy each reach experiences that conversation alone often can't. Many of my clients use both, and I weave in other proven approaches as each person needs them.

EMDR therapy

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing helps the brain finish processing memories that got stuck, so they lose their emotional charge. It follows a structured, well-researched eight-phase approach. We build safety and coping skills first, and you never have to retell every detail of what happened for it to help.

Art therapy

Some experiences live below language, in the body, in images and gestures. Drawing, painting and other simple materials give them a way out that doesn't depend on finding the right words first. It works for young children and adults alike, and no art experience or talent is needed.

Trauma comes in many forms

Trauma isn't only one terrible event. It can be a car accident or a medical scare. It can be growing up in a home that didn't feel safe, painful relationships, or years of feeling unseen. If something still affects how you feel, react, or relate to others, it's worth working through, however big or small it seems.

EMDR therapy

A structured, eight-phase approach that helps the brain reprocess distressing memories so they lose their intensity.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) is one of the most researched therapies for trauma. It’s recommended in trauma treatment guidelines from organizations including the World Health Organization and the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs.

It’s more than eye movements. EMDR includes a lot of conversation. We start with your history, build coping resources, and plan what to work on together. Only then do we begin reprocessing, using bilateral stimulation such as eye movements, alternating tones, or tapping. You stay present and in control, and you can pause at any time.

It isn’t only for major trauma. EMDR can also help with smaller, cumulative experiences that still shape how you react today, along with anxiety, phobias and performance fears.

Art therapy

Using drawing, painting, collage and other materials to reach and work through what's hard to put into words.

Art therapy is a mental health profession practiced by credentialed art therapists with graduate training in both psychotherapy and art. The artwork isn’t judged. It’s a way to notice, express, and organize experiences that talking alone can miss.

For every age. Children often express through images what they can’t yet say. Adults find it opens up material that years of talking hasn’t reached.

By telehealth. Sessions combine conversation with simple, guided art-making on camera. Basic supplies you already have at home, like paper, pencils or markers, are enough.

Play-based therapy

For children, play is how feelings get said. For grown-ups, it's how they finally get heard.

Young children rarely explain what’s wrong in sentences. They show it, in the stories they act out, the figures they choose, and the worlds they build. Play-based therapy meets them in that language, using toys, games, puppets, and art to help them make sense of big feelings and hard experiences.

Not just for kids. Teens and adults often find that a playful, hands-on approach lowers the pressure to “say it right.” Sometimes the part of us that needs healing is the part that never got to play.

By telehealth. Sessions use simple materials from home and interactive activities on screen, with parents or caregivers involved as needed for younger children.

Supporting approaches

Proven tools brought in as each person needs them, never as a checklist.

  • Internal Family Systems (IFS). Parts work that helps when someone feels stuck or overwhelmed by the hardest memories.
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Practical ways to notice and shift unhelpful thoughts and habits.
  • Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) skills. Tools for managing intense emotions and relationships.
  • Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT). Making room for hard feelings while moving toward what matters to you.
  • Somatic and mindfulness strategies. Body-based ways to calm the nervous system before, during, and after deeper work.
  • Attachment-focused work. Healing that pays attention to early relationships and how they shape us now.

About Elizabeth

Meeting you exactly where you are.

I am a Licensed Professional Counselor, board-certified art therapist (ATR-BC), EMDR Certified Therapist, and trauma-informed clinician with over two decades of experience in the mental health field. I am one of approximately 8,000 EMDR Certified Therapists nationwide who have completed the advanced training, clinical experience, and consultation required for certification.

My journey began with an undergraduate degree in fine arts, where I first discovered the powerful role creativity can play in expression, connection, and healing. Inspired to bring that healing potential into my clinical work, I went on to earn my graduate degree in Art Therapy and Counseling from Southwestern College in Santa Fe, New Mexico.

Throughout my career, I have worked in children’s behavioral health and trauma treatment centers, adult addiction treatment programs, partial hospitalization programs, outpatient mental health facilities serving individuals with severe and persistent mental health conditions, and group therapy settings. I helped develop an art therapy program and therapeutic groups for a children’s trauma treatment center. I also created and facilitated an art therapy group for a partial hospitalization drug and alcohol treatment program.

My specialized training includes studying under Nicole Porter Davis, creator of Emerald Sketch, a trauma-informed art therapy program that has been used globally to support individuals and communities affected by massacres and other traumatic events. These experiences have given me extensive knowledge of how trauma affects the mind, body, relationships, and sense of self, as well as how creative expression can support healing when words alone are not enough.

My primary therapeutic approaches include Art Therapy, EMDR, Internal Family Systems–informed therapy, somatic interventions, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, and Dialectical Behavior Therapy. Therapy is never one-size-fits-all, so I thoughtfully integrate different approaches based on each client’s unique needs, strengths, goals, and comfort level.

Although my knowledge, training, and clinical experience are extensive, I always find my way back to what matters most: meeting my clients exactly where they are. Our work will be collaborative, compassionate, and personalized. Together, we will create a treatment plan that supports your needs and helps you move toward meaningful, lasting healing, at a pace that feels right for you.

No Surprises Act

Your right to a Good Faith Estimate

Under the No Surprises Act, health care providers must give patients who don’t have insurance, or who are not using insurance, an estimate of the expected charges for medical services, including psychotherapy.

  • You have the right to receive a Good Faith Estimate of the total expected cost of any non-emergency services, including related costs such as tests, prescriptions, equipment and hospital fees.
  • Make sure your provider gives you a Good Faith Estimate in writing at least one business day before your service or item. You can also ask your provider for a Good Faith Estimate before you schedule.
  • If you receive a bill that is at least $400 more than your Good Faith Estimate, you can dispute the bill.
  • Make sure to save a copy or picture of your Good Faith Estimate.

For questions or more information about your right to a Good Faith Estimate, visit www.cms.gov/nosurprises or call 1-800-985-3059.

Not for emergencies

This website, email, and voicemail are not monitored for emergencies and are not a substitute for crisis care. If you or someone else is in danger, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. For a mental health crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), available 24 hours a day. The full list of crisis resources is below.

No therapist–client relationship

Reading this website, sending an email, or having a free consultation call does not create a therapist–client relationship. That relationship begins only after we mutually agree to work together and you complete intake, including informed consent.

Educational information only

Content on this site is general information about therapy and is not clinical advice, diagnosis, or treatment for any individual. Results of therapy vary from person to person, and no particular outcome is guaranteed.

Email, phone and text

Standard email and text messaging are not fully secure. Please use them only for scheduling and general questions, and avoid sharing personal health information. By choosing to contact me through these channels, you accept these limits on privacy. Clinical information is exchanged only through secure systems once we begin working together.

Website privacy

This website doesn’t use cookies, advertising pixels, or visitor tracking, and it has no forms that collect or store your information. If analytics are added in the future, this notice will be updated first.

Confidentiality

What you share in therapy is kept confidential and protected by federal and Pennsylvania law. Limits to confidentiality, such as situations involving risk of serious harm or suspected abuse of a child or vulnerable adult, will be explained in writing at intake.
